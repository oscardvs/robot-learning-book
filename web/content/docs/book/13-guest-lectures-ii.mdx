Chapter 12 covered the guest talks from weeks 2 through 6, which all circled the question of where
more robot data is going to come from. The five in this chapter, weeks 7 through 11, take the data
as given and ask what to build with it. The sourcing caveats set out at the start of Chapter 12
apply here unchanged: auto-generated captions checked line by line against reconstructed slides,
a speaker webcam occluding the top-right corner of every frame, and no recording at all for week
12\.

The five arrange themselves into an argument that gets progressively less settled. Ted Xiao gives
the ten-year history and shows how the field arrived at the current recipe. Scott Reed asks which
of three possible backbones that recipe should be built on, and declines to pick the incumbent.
Quan Vuong reports what the incumbent can actually do now, and one result that changes how you
should think about data quality. Archit Sharma comes from outside robotics with the axis that has
driven language models for two years and asks whether robotics can have it. And Lucas Beyer, in
the least robotic talk of the course, explains where the vision half of every vision-language-action
model came from — and closes with the most useful piece of research advice in either chapter.

## Ted Xiao: three eras, from arm farms to Gemini

Xiao spent most of the last decade at Google Brain and then Google DeepMind, and offers the history
as three eras. He is explicit that his examples skew toward where he worked and that plenty was
happening elsewhere, which is the right disclaimer and does not much weaken the arc.

**The era of existence proofs, roughly 2015 to 2021.** The founding observation was an asymmetry.
DQN and AlphaGo had shown that learning worked for sequential decision-making, but only in games.
Meanwhile the hardware for useful physical work already existed — Xiao points at the same
teleoperated Stanford demo of tidying a living room that Chapter 1 uses — so the bottleneck was
never the mechanism, it was the intelligence. If breakthroughs were happening in the digital domain
and the bodies were nearly ready, the intersection was worth attacking.

Google Brain's answer was to buy a dozen expensive robots when an academic lab had one, put them in
a room, and let them collect experience. These are the **arm farms**: banks of KUKAs grasping
objects out of bins under autonomous exploration policies. The work that came out of them is the
work Chapter 4 already covers from the other side. **QT-Opt** applied Q-learning at a scale that
forced every hard question at once — continuous high-dimensional action spaces, pixel observations,
no tabular anything — across more than **500,000 real grasps**. **RL-CycleGAN** is an early
real-to-sim evaluation protocol from the GAN era: build a digital twin, style-transfer its images
into something realistic, and evaluate there. They also had to make execution and inference run
concurrently, because a quasi-static formulation where the robot pauses to think is not what a
dynamic world affords.

Then the bet shifted toward imitation. **BC-Zero** collected a large teleoperated dataset on the
Everyday Robots one-arm mobile manipulator and trained a language-conditioned behavior-cloning
policy. **MT-Opt** scaled QT-Opt to many tasks. And **learning from play** — which Chapter 3 treats
— tried a different tack on diversity: rather than collecting task-specific rollouts, let people
simply play in an environment for a long time, and extract skills from the unstructured,
un-goal-directed result.

The era ends with a decision to stop incrementing. A new robot arrived at the start of COVID, and
rather than scale the existing effort they started over: a large contracting team, a much bigger
fleet, everyone collecting demonstrations in the same environments, and a target not of ten or
twenty tasks but **hundreds or thousands**, sustained for about eighteen months. To his knowledge
it remains one of the largest expert-demonstration collection projects ever run.

**The era of foundation models, roughly 2021 to 2024.** While that dataset was being built, the
language modelling world went through GPT-3, InstructGPT, PaLM, LaMDA and ChatGPT. Xiao decomposes
what robotics took from it into four moves: treat everything as tokens; reuse pre-trained
representations, or whole pre-trained models; use foundation models as components inside a larger
system; and shift from expensive online on-policy collection to **offline** learning on large
behavior-cloning datasets, where it stops mattering that the demonstration was not produced by your
own policy.

**RT-1** is those moves assembled: a strong vision encoder with FiLM conditioning, a token learner,
a transformer backbone, RGB and language in, **discretized action tokens** out, decoder-only.
**35 million parameters running at 3 Hz**, hundreds of tasks, success between roughly 60% and 80%.
Not perfect, and Xiao presents it as an existence proof rather than a product.

Then three different ways to use a foundation model, which are worth separating because the field
often runs them together.

_As a planner._ An LLM proposes plan steps and reports its log-probabilities; a value function
trained on top of RT-1 reports how feasible each candidate instruction is from the current state;
multiply the two and you get plans grounded in what the robot can actually do. The language model
likes "pick up the apple"; the policy knows it must go find one first.

_As a data-augmentation engine._ RT-1's dataset was 87,000 trajectories gathered over eighteen
months — tiny by present standards — and, more to the point, poor in _instruction_ diversity. A
VLM relabels episodes with other task descriptions they could plausibly satisfy, enriching the
language distribution for free. Xiao notes this is now standard practice for training image and
video models: synthetic captioning that arbitrages what the VLM knows against what your dataset
lacks.

_As the backbone itself._ **RT-2** treats action prediction as visual question answering — emit
language tokens that decode into actions — on PaLI-X and PaLM-E, co-fine-tuned roughly **50/50**
with internet data. What transferred was the part nobody had put in the robot data: new objects,
common-sense reasoning, even reading text in the scene.

**Open X-Embodiment** closes the era, pooling and cleaning data from more than thirty labs
collected for unrelated projects on unrelated robots, to test whether motion knowledge transfers
across morphologies at all.

**The era of scale, 2024 to now.** Gemini displaces PaLM and PaLI as the backbone, and the bimanual
ALOHA puppeteering rig raises both throughput and quality of data. **Gemini Robotics** combines
Gemini 2.0's world knowledge with large-scale robot action, showing dexterity, post-training onto
new embodiments — a Unitree humanoid, a bimanual Franka — and compositional generalization at test
time. **Gemini Robotics 1.5** adds two things Xiao separates carefully: **thinking**, meaning
language-based chain-of-thought reasoning before acting, and **motion transfer**, meaning all
embodiments are trained together at pre-training time in a shared action space, so one network
drives all of them with no per-embodiment fine-tuning.

Beyond Alphabet he points to π0.6's long-horizon espresso making, and to two structural problems.
**Evaluation has become a bottleneck in its own right**: thousands to tens of thousands of real
trials are needed for a statistically meaningful A/B test, which is why simulation evaluation and
decentralized efforts like **RoboArena** exist. And **data scaling has split into competing,
opinionated bets** that he lays out without adjudicating: co-designed glove hardware shipped
globally, in the UMI lineage (Sunday, Generalist); skipping the glove and capturing pure egocentric
hand data with a GoPro, an iPhone or glasses; and in-the-wild deployment data.

His closing note is the one worth carrying forward. Everything he discussed is manipulation.
Locomotion, balance and whole-body control advanced in the same decade by an entirely different
route — sim-to-real reinforcement learning — and produced robots that dance, do kung fu and recover
from shoves. "That's not happening via imitation learning." Uniting the two paradigms is open, and
nobody in these ten talks claims to know how.

  Xiao's summary of the decade is worth quoting because it is the most
  defensible thing anyone in the guest track says about where the field stands: "we've made more
  progress than many of us would ever have thought would be possible in our lifetimes, but now
  there's more open questions and uncertainties and open frontiers than ever."

In the Q\&A he is asked whether high-quality teleoperation data will survive. His answer is a
timeline argument rather than a technical one. Teleop has been the gold standard because data
quality is so hard to quantify that maximizing it in-house was the only safe bet. That burden is
now being externalized to distributions unlike the deployment target, and he expects the field to
trend toward an NVIDIA-style data pyramid — internet, simulation and non-robot video at the base, a
thin layer of fine-tuning data on top. But: "if it takes 10 years, that means that in the meantime
it's probably better to prioritize really high quality teleop data." The research question is
which, and the answer depends on a schedule nobody controls.

## Scott Reed: which backbone, and what a backbone is for

Reed led the Gato paper at DeepMind and now works on the GEAR team at NVIDIA. His definition is
worth adopting because it is sharper than the usual one: the **backbone** is the architecture that
encodes sensors and language into a shared representation for action prediction, and what makes the
choice consequential is not inference-time behavior but **how the policy absorbs knowledge during
pre-training and carries it to downstream tasks.** Three candidates: train from scratch, start from
a vision-language model, or start from a video model.

### Vision-language-action models

The incumbent. RT-2, OpenVLA, the π series, NVIDIA's GR00T models, TRI's Large Behavior Model,
Gemini. The virtues are real: one modality for everything, good cross-embodiment support, direct
fine-tuning of a VLM into control tokens, and internet-scale grounding.

Reed walks the architectures from simple to elaborate. **OpenVLA** is the clean case — pre-trained
vision encoders (DINO, CLIP, or both), a pre-trained language model, actions discretized by binning
and de-tokenized back to continuous values at execution, with much of it frozen initially. The
**π** models are more involved: discrete _and_ continuous action prediction, a FAST tokenizer, and
separate pathways so the VLM can be co-trained on language tasks such as subtask prediction
alongside actions.

The reason for that separation is a failure mode he explains carefully, and it is the most useful
engineering detail in the talk. If you naively co-train discrete language-token prediction while
back-propagating flow-matching or diffusion gradients through the VLM, **you destroy the VLM's
weights** and lose properties like instruction following — catastrophic forgetting, arriving
through the gradient path rather than the data. Keeping everything discrete avoids it and opens up
pre-training options. But a separate diffusion or flow **action expert** still earns its place,
and for a reason that is not about accuracy: decoding discrete action tokens can produce **decoding
errors**, and an undefined decoded action that falls through to a default position target could
fling an arm across the room. The action expert never has decoding errors. Safety is the argument.

He singles out the **TRI Large Behavior Model** paper as the one everyone should read, and not for
its architecture. It tests rigorously whether multi-task VLA-style pre-training actually improves
downstream performance, data efficiency and top-end performance, using **double-blind evaluation** —
paired policies, evaluators who do not know which is which, head-to-head on the same robot at the
same time — with real statistical analysis. The findings confirm what the field believed. The point
is that somebody finally checked, against the difficulty of real-robot evaluation that Chapter 9
and Gupta's talk both flag.

The weaknesses he lists are unusually frank for a survey of one's own field. Prediction and
reasoning happen in the _action_ space, which may be the wrong place to reason about physics. The
action representation is bolted on after pre-training. Fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting.
And two structural problems: **data islands**, where throwing every embodiment into one mixture and
calling it cross-embodiment may just produce disjoint regions with little transfer between them,
given how different the forms and correlational structures are; and the fact that evidence for
zero-shot out-of-distribution or new-embodiment generalization is, in his stated opinion, "limited
or non-existent" for contemporary VLAs.

### World action models

<Figure id="fig-wams" number="13.1" src="/slides/guest07_reed/slide_004.jpg" scale="88%" videoUrl="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fqkp_wkov6M&t=798s" timecode="13:18" source="Reed">
  The world-action-model paradigm as Reed frames it: current VLAs memorize actions rather than reasoning about physics, so predict world state in pixels before acting, and treat pixel space as the universal action space. Credit: reconstructed from the guest-lecture recording, Scott Reed, week 8.
</Figure>

The challenger, and the acronym is **WAM** (<XRef kind="fig" anchor="fig-wams" number="13.1" />). The examples are **DreamZero** from NVIDIA
GEAR, which the slide credits with first place on DROID RoboArena _without any robot-data
pre-training_; **Cosmos Policy**, also GEAR; and ByteDance's **GR-1** and **GR-2**, autoregressive
interleaving models that got there earlier than most.

The paradigm shift the slide states in three lines: current VLAs memorize actions rather than
reasoning about physics; so predict world state in pixels before or jointly with acting; and treat
pixel space as the universal action space. Reed adds the argument he finds most convincing, which
is about pre-training rather than inference — predicting future pixels lets the model **absorb far
more from the same data** than predicting actions alone. "We can just squeeze a lot more juice out
of the data that way."

Architecturally, DreamZero encodes frames to latents with a VAE, concatenates action embeddings,
conditions on language, and runs a **block-causal DiT** so that key-value caching makes inference
tractable, with joint flow matching over both the action chunk and the future frame chunk. A
"Flash" variant takes one or two steps without fully denoising the future vision chunk, focusing
compute on the action chunk, and performs just as well — which is itself informative about how much
of the video prediction is needed at test time. Across the family there are three ways to get an
action out: fully sample a rollout and recover actions with an inverse dynamics model; plan; or use
video prediction purely as a pre-training objective and never diffuse observations at inference at
all. Reed draws the analogy to reasoning models explicitly: much of the benefit comes from having
done it during pre-training, because the representations are better, even when you skip it later.

**Cosmos Policy** additionally diffuses future proprioception and value, which he thinks gives it a
stronger claim to being a world model proper, and its design tiles proprioception, action and value
embeddings spatially into the image embedding space so that from the diffusion model's point of
view everything is simply vision.

Two empirical findings are the ones to keep. **Scale helps**: sample quality and success rate both
rose substantially from the 2B model to the 14B. And the failure analysis: **most failures stem
from video-generation errors rather than action prediction** — whatever the video predicted is what
the policy did. That is an unusually actionable result, because it says improvements to the video
backbone translate directly into policy performance, and the data needed to make them does not have
to be robot data. An audience member pushes on exactly the right point — perhaps the action
conditioning is what is broken, not the video — and Reed concedes the decomposition matters, says
they did correlate task failure with low-quality video, and expects the correlation to tighten as
video models improve, while allowing that very fine-grained manipulation may genuinely not be
recoverable from the video signal.

Weaknesses: expensive to sample; modelling every pixel is probably overkill, since the latent space
was trained to reconstruct aesthetically convincing video rather than to control anything; a
domain-specific tokenizer can be far more compressed and still generalize; and context length is
currently limited.

### From scratch, and why it is interesting again

Historically this meant a bespoke network per task, then one big network on all the data — **Gato**,
which serialized text, images, question answering, Atari and robotics into one flat token sequence
for one transformer, and **RT-1**, **RT-X** and **Octo**. Data islands again, and a ceiling set by
how much high-quality data a consortium or a determined individual can assemble.

What makes it live again is that the data changed. **UMI-style large-scale human pre-training** —
Generalist, Sunday — reaches a scale Reed cites as around **500,000 hours** of sensorized human
data. And the camera-only route: **Ego4D** at about **20,000 hours** of in-the-wild recording, plus
roughly **50 hours** of sensorized data (Vive trackers on the wrist, Manus gloves on the fingers)
and a small amount of robot data. Both show scaling-law behavior in _human_ data: validation loss
falls, success rate rises monotonically. So a foundation model need not be chained to a VLM or a
video model at all, and the architecture may not matter much.

His conclusion is a bet on the combination — world action models for their generalization
properties, plus scaling human data — and he says so with appropriate hedging about being tongue in
cheek.

Two Q\&A answers are worth keeping. On whether world models make simulators redundant: not soon.
Neural simulation may shine for contact-rich manipulation and deformables, but hand-crafted
simulators are so efficient and world models so expensive to run that for whole-body control on
humanoids and quadrupeds he doubts simulators are going anywhere. On tactile and force sensing, he
makes a LeCun-style argument: a video predictor good enough to drive down its loss must latently
represent forces, because there are correlates of them in the pixels. So we may need _some_
sensorized data, but perhaps orders of magnitude less than the video, and mainly in order to
**elicit** features the model already has rather than to teach them.

## Quan Vuong: what a single checkpoint can do

Vuong co-founded Physical Intelligence and previously started and co-led RT-X and Open X-Embodiment
at Google DeepMind. He kept the talk deliberately informal, and the claim is narrower and more
checkable than the title suggests: the innovation in **π0.7** is **one model that performs many
tasks at a high level**, where previously high performance on a task meant post-training a
mediocre general model onto it. A single checkpoint matters because it is the thing that scales —
no question at deployment of which checkpoint or which hyperparameters go with which task.

He frames scaling as two axes, data and model, and then immediately complicates it. Train on all
your robot data and you will usually get something worse than a heavily-tuned specialist. **More
data does not equal a better model**, and most of the talk is about why not.

The architecture is largely inherited from π0.5 and π0.6: a language model and a pre-trained vision
encoder, with an action expert grafted on — his phrase is that it teaches the model "to speak the
robot's language". The inputs are the interesting part. A prompt ("clean the kitchen"), optionally
a **subtask**, and **metadata describing the speed and quality of the training episode**. New in
π0.7 is **goal conditioning**, which he credits for much of the improved generalization. A separate
high-level component — a human, a high-level policy, or a world model — supplies plans but cannot
itself produce low-level actions.

The task suite is chosen to stress different axes, and all of it runs from a single pre-training
checkpoint: laundry folding, for an effectively infinite state space; driving a tiny screw into a
robot arm, for precision; building a box, for two-handed coordination; and taking out the trash,
for horizon.

Against an **RL specialist** and an **SFT specialist** tuned per task, π0.7 matches or beats both.
The explanation he gives for beating them is the sentence from this talk most worth remembering:

<Aside>
  In large-scale robotic evaluation, really every evaluation is a generalization evaluation.
</Aside>

You fix the scene as carefully as you can and something always changes anyway, and the generalist
copes better with whatever that is.

**The data-quality result.** The plot has the fraction of training data on the <InlineMath tex={"x"} />-axis, ordered so
that moving right adds progressively _lower-quality_ data. Without metadata conditioning, going
from 80% to 100% — adding the worst data — makes performance **worse**. With the speed-and-quality
metadata, the same addition produces a **large jump**. The lesson generalizes past π0.7: data that
hurts a model when presented flat can help it when the model is told what it is looking at. This is
a different claim from "filter your data", and a more useful one.

**Cross-environment transfer** is the result he says surprised them. A checkpoint trained on
shirt-folding data from one bimanual station runs shirt folding on a station of two UR5s, with **no
shirt-folding data from that station at all** — an unseen combination of task and robot, on a task
that normally takes hundreds of hours because it is precise and the observation space is enormous.
Separately, a human coaching the robot in language gets tasks done in unseen environments on unseen
objects; his example involves air-frying a sweet potato, where neither the food nor the air fryer
appears in the training data.

On externalization: π0 and π0.5 are open source, and the released checkpoints are **the same ones
Physical Intelligence researchers use internally** — a question he says surprises people. The
deployment examples are the most concrete evidence in either chapter that any of this works outside
a lab. Weave Robotics folding laundry on real customer orders in a laundromat in the Mission in San
Francisco, on clothing the model has never seen, recovering from tangled trouser legs. And a
packaging robot loading deformable items into soft pouches through a narrow gap — a task he points
out is not easily automated with a classical stack, because the pouch is deformable and errors
happen. The detail he likes is that the video starts in daylight and ends at night: a full-day
workload, mostly autonomous. The economic frame he offers is **remote intervention rate** — can you
reach autonomy high enough to break even, and therefore to scale? For some partner tasks, he says,
they are essentially there.

Three answers from the Q\&A. On **multi-finger hands**: for scale you need hardware that is cheap,
reliable and easy to maintain, and today a multi-finger hand gives you **two of the three** — one
costs as much as six or seven two-finger systems, or is cheap and breaks constantly. Across
thousands of realistic tasks at PI they have rarely needed more than two fingers, so hands are a
distraction _if_ the question you are asking is about intelligence. On **home robots**: the dream is
real and he wants to build one, but a home robot is close to physical-AI-complete, consumer
robotics is economically brutal, human preferences vary enormously, and the binding constraint is
model capability. They show home tasks anyway because they are an excellent research testbed _and_
legible — a layperson cannot tell that a precise assembly task is hard, but everyone understands
laundry. On **model size**: currently single-digit billions, and he expects low, then mid, then high
double-digit billions, with more emergent capability at each step.

## Archit Sharma: the other axis

Sharma is a research scientist at Google DeepMind, co-created **DPO**, and led **Gemini Deep
Think**. His talk is the only one in the guest track that is not about robots, and it is included
because the axis it describes is the one Chapter 10 is about.

The history in one line: until 2024, gains in language models came from scaling **pre-training**
compute — next-token prediction, more data, larger models, a remarkably scalable objective. Late
2024 and early 2025 brought **reasoning models**, which spend time thinking, working through
context and tool calls before answering. The new axis is **test-time compute**, and performance
rises with it _predictably_, which is what makes it a scaling axis rather than a trick.

The evidence is from the Gemini 2.5 report. **AIME 2025** — a high-school competition with
final-answer problems that language models were terrible at before 2024 — improves by over **twenty
percentage points** as thinking time grows. **LiveCodeBench** and **GPQA Diamond** move the same
way. And large fractions of the Gemini 1.5 → 2.5 Pro improvements on maths, coding and science are
attributable to inference-time scaling rather than a bigger pre-train.

His definition is worth having precisely: an ordinary network spends a **fixed** amount of forward
computation per input, whereas a test-time-compute system runs some kind of **search** — in output
space or elsewhere — and can spend far more than a single forward pass.

**AlphaGo is the canonical instance.** The raw parametric model with one forward pass reaches about
**3,000 Elo**. Add test-time search — **capped at five seconds** — and the _same model_ reaches
about **5,000 Elo**. He glosses the scale: 100 Elo points is roughly a 64% win rate, a 2,000-point
gap means the weaker player essentially never wins, and by 3,500 you are already superhuman. The
caveat he states himself is that this is hand-crafted for a game with a simulator.

<EditorNote>
  Chapter 10 discusses AlphaGo's test-time search using the main lecture's own
  framing — a 120-Elo rule of thumb and a slide claiming a 100,000× factor — and carries an
  editor's note on the arithmetic. Sharma's numbers are a _different_ presentation of the same
  system: 3,000 to 5,000 Elo under a five-second search cap. They are reported here as his and are
  not merged with the main lecture's figures, which were read from a different slide.
</EditorNote>

**Chain of thought** is the bridge to language models, demonstrated with the canonical
Roger-and-the-tennis-balls prompt: forced to answer directly, the model fails; encouraged to work
step by step, it succeeds. **GRPO** and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards then made
the behavior emerge rather than be prompted: warm-start a model to reason, run RL on domains where
the answer can be checked — maths with known answers, code with unit tests — and the average
response length grows _on its own_ while training accuracy climbs from roughly 15–25% to 75–80%.
The model teaches itself to spend more compute. Chapter 10 develops GRPO's objective in full; what
Sharma adds is the observation that the response-length curve is the thing to watch.

The other axes for spending test-time compute, which he lists without ranking: reasoning several
times in parallel, iterative self-refinement over previous answers, tools such as web search and
code execution, sandboxes, and self-verification. These produce both general reasoning systems
(Deep Think, ChatGPT Pro) and specific harnesses (Codex, Gemini Deep Research, Claude Code).

**Deep Think** is where the numbers get striking. On IMO-level proof problems, with inference
compute on a **log scale** and proof correctness graded by an expert human, accuracy rises from
about **40% to about 90%**, with the model working for hours. It was the first system to reach
**gold-medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad**, a milestone not expected in
2025\. On Codeforces the latest version reached an Elo around **3,445**, with only about **seven
humans in the world** rated higher. And the benefit is not confined to verifiable domains: asked to
produce an HTML voxel scene of cherry blossoms and pagodas, the jump from 2.5 Flash to 2.5 Pro
improves it, but _scaling inference compute_ improves it considerably further, with visibly more
detail in the trees and the pagoda.

The open problems he names are the useful part of the talk for a robotics audience.

**Efficiency.** Inference scaling gives predictable gains; using tokens and tools _well_ is wide
open.

**Depth.** A reframing he says is not immediately obvious: much of pre-training's success came from
increasing model depth, and inference-time scaling also increases the **depth of computation**,
since more tokens means more forward passes. So the real question is what the most effective way to
add depth is, and tokens are only one answer.

**Long-horizon agents.** As agents work for days or weeks, how do you keep them making progress,
measure that they are, and keep it safe? He argues the demand is obviously real — a million dollars
of compute is trivially justified against the Riemann hypothesis or a drug-discovery problem — and
that companies which once scaled headcount may instead scale inference compute.

**Knowledge discovery.** Models solve well-defined problems superbly and struggle to work out
_which problems matter_. He cites the proposed **Einstein test**: rediscover general relativity
from data available only up to the early twentieth century.

**Improving with experience.** People get better at their jobs; agents redo the same expensive
reasoning every time. Memories and skills are early and still largely manual versions of a fix, and
he thinks there is a great deal of headroom.

On scaling RLVR to harder domains, his answer is three constraints, and it is the most practical
thing said about RL infrastructure in the course. The infrastructure itself is "incredibly hard" —
models must train stably, and **training/inference mismatch can compound over training into serious
divergence**. Verifiable problems **run out**: you start with easy ones, and against a strong
pre-trained model easy problems stop carrying signal quickly. And verification itself gets harder
where it matters most — maths **proofs** are not easy to verify, and depending on how you do it,
the reward can be hacked.

## Lucas Beyer: where the vision half came from

Beyer describes his own talk as "probably going to be the least robotics lecture in this robot
learning course", and the justification is straightforward: every VLA in Chapters 9 and 13 is built
on a VLM, and a great many of them on **PaliGemma** specifically. He worked on ViT, SigLIP and
PaliGemma at Google Brain in Zürich and is now at Meta. His long-term goal, stated at the start and
returned to at the end, is a robot that any non-technical person can teach to do anything, without
programming.

He organizes the modern recipe as four stages — **pre-train, mid-train, fine-tune, RL-tune** — and
walks the first three.

### Pre-training, and three lessons that cost the field years

He begins by demonstrating general perception on the audience: three-way few-shot classification of
flowers (easy — you have seen millions), then satellite images (fewer), then a final unfamiliar
domain most of the room has never encountered, which they still get right. That capability is the
target, and to make progress on it you have to measure it: **VTAB**, the Visual Task Adaptation
Benchmark — pre-train however you like, then adapt to a long, diverse list of tasks from a few
examples each, and average.

The first finding is negative and important. They reimplemented a large subset of self-supervised
pre-training methods and found **no method is consistently best**, but that **making the model
larger is always better**. Worse, plotting pre-training performance against downstream performance
gives a scatter with **almost no correlation across methods** — a trend appears only _within_ a
method as it scales. He flags this explicitly as something that should transfer to robotics, and it
is a sharp warning: your pre-training metric is not telling you what you think.

The consequence is practical. To choose a model you must evaluate it, and evaluation needs
examples — either a validation set or you watching the robot and writing down good and bad, which
_is_ a validation set. So why not put a few of those labelled examples into the pre-training? They
did, and transfer improved dramatically. That killed purely self-supervised pre-training for the
team and moved them to supervised pre-training at scale.

The scaling result that followed — the **BiT** work, which he calls his second-favourite plot — is
the one that matters. Contemporary papers scaling models on ImageNet-1M crawled from about 50 to
about 55, which is not worth the cost of a much larger model. Going to roughly 15× more
pre-training data, and then another 20× beyond that, changes the picture entirely: the largest
model on the largest data is far better than anything published before. **ObjectNet**, which places
ImageNet classes into deliberately strange poses and locations to test out-of-distribution
generalization, shows exactly the same trend, which is the stronger version of the claim.

Why did this take the community years? Three lessons.

**You need all three of larger model, larger data and longer training, each by orders of
magnitude.** Two of the three gives much less; one gives essentially nothing. Concretely: take the
standard ResNet-50-on-ImageNet recipe, train it on 10× more data, and results get _worse_ — a
negative result several blog posts reported at the time, and one his team reproduced. Train 10×
longer, and then another 10× longer, and it starts to pay.

**Patience.** His favourite plot in his career is a training curve whose first **eight GPU-weeks**
look completely flat. Any reasonable person kills that job and reuses the hardware. Zoomed out to
**eight GPU-months** it is still clearly improving. "Good thing we were not reasonable."

**Details that are invisible at small scale.** He shows two weight-decay settings where the run you
would kill on the evidence of two short preliminary curves is the one that wins at the end.

### Where the data comes from, and what filtering destroys

Classical pre-training data is class-list-first: invent a list of classes, then crawl images for
each, forever bounded by the imagination of whoever wrote the list. His anecdote is the best in the
course: **MS COCO's 80 classes** came from the senior professor on the project asking his eight- to
ten-year-old child what things they could think of — microwave, pizza, frisbee, baseball — so
essentially every object detector you have used was trained to recognize, in his phrase, "typical 8
to 10 year old North American things".

**CLIP** inverts it: list _images_ by crawling the web and use whatever text surrounds them, most
canonically alt-text. You get some garbage, and you also get "Frankfurt airport skyline", which
nobody would ever have put on a class list. He teaches the mechanism in full because half the room
did not know it: put around 32,000 image-text pairs in a batch, encode both sides to vectors, and
for each text classify which image vector it matches and vice versa — a softmax over dot products
with the diagonal as ground truth. **SigLIP** simplifies this to a per-pair binary decision:
dot product, sigmoid, target 0 or 1.

Then the argument he clearly cares most about. Having gained the ability to learn every concept on
the web, the field proceeded to throw it away by **filtering**: keep only words appearing in English
Wikipedia; use LAION's 2-billion English-only subset rather than the full 5 billion; score pairs
with the original English-only CLIP model and keep the high scorers; keep images that resemble
ImageNet. Each step improves the number — measured on ImageNet, which is itself North-American
biased. In a controlled experiment, the English-filtered model identifies the **Milad Tower in
Tehran** as the **CN Tower in Toronto**, and places a Brazilian cathedral in Montreal; the
unfiltered model gets both right. Then he asks the room to close their eyes and think of a toilet,
and points out that what they pictured is one design among several, and the others do not survive
North-American filtering. They built a benchmark showing that as models improve on ImageNet and
COCO they get _worse_ on across-the-globe coverage, and campaigned against the practice.

### What contrastive learning cannot learn, and the lesson of the talk

There is a structural limitation in CLIP-style training, and the way he explains it is the clearest
in the course. Take a perfectly clean image-caption pair. Within a mini-batch, the model only has to
do _enough_ to pick the right partner. If no other cat image is in the batch, recognizing "cat"
suffices and the rest of the caption can be ignored entirely. To be forced to learn _sitting_, the
batch must contain a cat and a dog that are not sitting. To learn _left of_, it must contain a cat
and a dog sitting the other way round. From random web data, at batch size 32,000 or even a
million, that is vanishingly unlikely. **So CLIP does not learn relations.**

People patched this by constructing counterpart examples and enumerating relations. Beyer wanted a
fix that scales, and the simple one is a **captioning loss**: generate the caption word by word, so
that producing "left" rather than "right" after "sitting" is forced by the objective. The original
CLIP paper contained a figure showing captioning was far less efficient; his team reproduced it and
found it an artifact of that particular setup. With the fix, the relation benchmarks are nailed. His
practical recommendation: **SigLIP 2 has a captioning loss; use it instead of SigLIP.**

And then the lesson he says to take away if you take only one thing from the whole lecture.

<Aside>
  Look at the data.
</Aside>

The relation benchmark asks you to choose which of two sentences matches a picture: _the horse is
eating the grass_, or _the grass is eating the horse_. You do not need the picture. The benchmark is
full of these. To prove the point they trained a **blind captioner** that never sees the image, and
it nails the benchmark too. He notes this class of broken benchmark recurs roughly every five years
— "almost every generation of PhDs" — and that others later built a properly controlled version, on
which the captioning pre-training also wins.

### Mid-training, and what a training mixture is for

Mid-training is a recent term, and the definition he endorses is: the steps after the bulk of
self-supervised pre-training, shaping **core capabilities**, before fine-tuning or RL for exact
behaviors. **PaliGemma is the canonical example.** An image encoder — CLIP or SigLIP — turns the
image into vectors, which are simply tokens; those are concatenated with text tokens, typically a
question or a task; a language model emits the answer. In a VLA, actions come out instead. It counts
as mid-training because it stitches two pre-trained models together and installs skills that neither
pre-training supplied.

What matters is the **training mixture**, and the design principle is that a mixture item does not
have to be useful to a user — it has to force a skill. Captioning. **OCR**, implemented as: feed
random images containing text and ask for the whole text, or chunks of it. Nobody wants that as a
product, but it installs _reading_. Object-presence questions. **Inverting question and answer** —
given the answer, generate the likely question. Detection, segmentation, and region captioning.
Several of these were added specifically to make PaliGemma good for robotics-style tasks, so it
learns about objects and locations, which is why so many VLAs are built on it. Mid-training is also
where you scale the axes you could not afford earlier: input resolution, video context length, 3D
slices.

Turning tasks into text is what makes this uniform. Detection becomes text by binning each axis into
roughly **1024** bins — near pixel resolution — and emitting bounding boxes as coordinate tokens.
The general mechanism is **VQ-VAE**, whose discrete bottleneck dictionary can be added to the
model's vocabulary, so a segmentation mask becomes a sequence of tokens the VLM can emit inline with
text. Fine-tuning is then a large mixture of specialized tasks, and it produces **skill transfer**:
a model that learned segmentation on natural photographs and question answering on satellite images
can segment satellite images, a combination present in neither stage.

### Is perception solved?

He says, slightly provocatively, that this recipe "somewhat solves perception" — from barely working
eight years ago to essentially solved. The obvious objection is the "VLMs are blind" literature,
which shows frontier models failing at tasks like counting the intersections of two kinked lines,
where humans exceed 90%.

His answer is a distinction worth adopting. _Solved_ means **the recipe** is solved; you still have
to apply it. Those obscure tasks are simply not in anyone's supervised fine-tuning mixture, and
there is no reason they would be. So he generated **128 examples** of the line-intersection task and
fine-tuned PaliGemma to **95–96%**; noting it is really a classification problem, he fine-tuned
**SigLIP alone to about 97%**. The models are not blind. 128 examples cannot teach the underlying
skill of finding intersections — they are enough to teach the model **what the user wants**.

The recipe as advice, then: for any _perception_ task, spend an afternoon collecting a couple of
hundred examples, fine-tune a good pre-trained VLM, tune the learning rate, and it will work.

The caveat is where robot learning lives. The recipe does not solve what comes after perception:
**reasoning** ("solve my homework"; "explain why this is funny") and **planning** ("robot, make me a
cappuccino" — it knows perfectly well where the machine and the cup are, and cannot sequence the
task). And he names the unsatisfying part explicitly: free-form text alone is not enough to specify
a new task, and you still need a little fine-tuning to communicate one.

Four Q\&A answers are worth recording. **Augmentations** buy roughly one order of magnitude of
effective data and no more, after expensive tuning — if you can get more data, get more data.
**Deduplication** is done heavily and silently in all their work: remove anything close to your
evaluation tasks from pre-training, or your benchmarks are in your training set and you are not
measuring generalization at all. His figure is roughly **10 million near-duplicates per billion
pre-training images**, which costs nothing in coverage and buys the right to believe your own
results. **Do not freeze the image encoder** — PaliGemma does not, and most VLMs that do cannot
recover from CLIP's limitations, which matters exactly for relations and locations; he would not use
a frozen-encoder VLM for fine-tuning. And **distillation** is the missing addendum to his own recipe:
pre-train, transfer, RL-tune to make the model as good as it can be, and then distil it down to the
latency the robot needs. It is expensive because it needs very long training, and it can be the
difference between a blocker and something you can actually run.

## Where this breaks

Set these five talks beside each other and the field does not agree with itself, in ways that are
easy to miss when each result is read alone.

**Nobody agrees on the backbone.** Reed lays out three options and bets against the incumbent,
saying zero-shot generalization evidence for VLAs is "limited or non-existent". Vuong reports a VLA
generalizing to an unseen task-and-robot combination, and is himself surprised. Xiao thinks video
priors are clearly a rising trend and doubts they replace text. These are the same weeks of the
same course.

**The status of world models is genuinely unsettled.** Reed's failure analysis says most WAM
failures are video-generation failures, which he reads as encouraging. An audience member offers the
alternative reading — that the action conditioning is what does not work — and the honest answer is
that the two have not been decisively separated.

**Test-time scaling may or may not transfer.** Sharma's evidence is overwhelming within language,
and rests on a property robotics lacks: cheap, automatic verification. His own list of obstacles —
verifiable problems running out, proofs being hard to verify, reward hacking — is worse in robotics,
where verifying an outcome means running a robot. Kumar's RaC in Chapter 12 is the one result in
either chapter that produces a test-time-scaling curve for robots, and it does it by redefining the
axis as recoveries rather than thoughts.

**And the field's own benchmarks may not be measuring what they claim.** Beyer's blind captioner is
the sharpest single result in these two chapters, precisely because it is not about capability at
all. A benchmark the community used to demonstrate a limitation of CLIP could be solved without
looking at the image. His deduplication figures point the same way: if you have not removed near
duplicates, your generalization numbers are partly memorization, and almost nobody reports whether
they did.

## What this connects to

Xiao's three eras are a reading guide to the second half of this book: era one is Chapters 4 and 5,
era two is Chapters 7 and 9, and era three is Chapters 9, 10 and 11. His closing observation that
locomotion advanced by an entirely different route is the gap Chapter 11 also leaves open.

Reed's talk is a direct commentary on Chapters 7, 8 and 9 — it takes the backbone question those
chapters answer implicitly and makes it explicit, and his data-islands argument is the strongest
available objection to the cross-embodiment optimism of Chapter 9.

Vuong's results are Chapter 9's π-series brought up to date, and his metadata finding belongs beside
Chapter 3's discussion of data quality, because it says something Chapter 3 does not: that quality
can be _conditioned on_ rather than filtered for.

Sharma's talk is Chapter 10 from the language side, and the two should be read together — Chapter
10 develops GRPO's objective, and Sharma supplies the empirical picture of what scaling that
objective's inference budget actually buys, along with the reasons it is hard.

Beyer's talk is the prehistory of Chapter 7. ViT, CLIP, SigLIP and the tokenization strategies
Chapter 7 presents as the machinery of sequence modelling are here presented as a decade of
argument about data, and <XRef kind="fig" anchor="fig-wams" number="13.1" />'s alternative — pixels rather than text as the universal space —
is the live challenge to all of it.
